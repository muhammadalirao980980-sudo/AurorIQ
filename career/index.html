The framework

Six themes that describe work interests

Psychologist John Holland's RIASEC model is the backbone of modern career-interest testing. Most people are a blend of a few themes, not one — and that blend is what the profiler surfaces.

  • R

    Realistic

    Hands-on and practical — building, fixing, and working with tools, machines, plants, or animals. The "doers" who like tangible results.

  • I

    Investigative

    Analytical and curious — researching, experimenting, and solving abstract problems. The "thinkers" drawn to ideas and evidence.

  • A

    Artistic

    Creative and expressive — designing, writing, and performing without rigid rules. The "creators" who value originality and freedom.

  • S

    Social

    People-centered — teaching, advising, and caring for others. The "helpers" energized by human connection and service.

  • E

    Enterprising

    Persuasive and driven — leading, selling, and taking charge. The "persuaders" who like influence, risk, and ambition.

  • C

    Conventional

    Organized and precise — structuring data, records, and processes. The "organizers" who bring order and accuracy to complexity.

The honest part

What an interest test can and can't do

Interest inventories are genuinely useful, and they're also routinely oversold — so here's the honest version. What they do well is surface patterns in what energizes you that you might not have put into words. Seeing "Investigative" and "Artistic" rise to the top can reframe a vague restlessness into something you can act on. What they cannot do is tell you what you'd be good at, what will pay your bills, or what you should choose. Interest is not ability: plenty of people love an activity they're mediocre at, and tolerate ones they excel at. And a career is chosen inside real constraints — money, location, family, timing, luck — that no questionnaire can see.

So we treat the result as a starting map, not a destination. Your top themes point toward families of work worth investigating; the way to test them is in the world — shadowing, side projects, conversations with people who do the job — not in another quiz. Used that way, a five-minute profile can save you months of drifting. Used as an oracle, it just adds a new way to feel boxed in. We'd rather build the first kind of tool.

It also helps to read a Holland Code the way it's meant to be read: as a blend, not a label. The three letters matter in combination, and the gaps between them matter too. Someone who scores Investigative 82, Artistic 78, and Social 40 has a very different profile from someone whose top three sit at 60, 58, and 55 — the first person has two strong pulls and a clear drop-off, the second is a genuine generalist whose energy spreads evenly. The order of the letters hints at emphasis, but two adjacent themes with nearly identical scores are effectively tied, and both are worth exploring. Themes that sit near each other on Holland's hexagon — Artistic and Investigative, or Social and Enterprising — tend to combine comfortably, while opposite themes like Realistic and Social pull in different directions, which is useful to notice when a role seems to demand both. None of this is destiny; it's simply a more careful way to read what the numbers are telling you before you go and test them against real work.

Questions

About this category

What does the Career category measure?

Vocational interests — the kinds of work and activity you're drawn to — using the RIASEC model. The interest profiler, a work-values profiler, and a work-style profiler are live today, with a strengths instrument in development. It maps interests, not ability, and frames results as directions to explore.

Can a test really tell me what career to pick?

No test can, and we don't claim to. Interest inventories surface patterns in what energizes you, which is one genuinely useful input — but career fit also depends on your skills, values, circumstances, and the realities of the job market. Treat the results as a map of directions worth exploring, not an answer.

Is the RIASEC model scientifically sound?

RIASEC, or Holland's model, is one of the most researched and widely used frameworks in vocational psychology, and it holds up well as a description of interest structure. Its limits are honest ones: it describes interests, not talent or success, and people are always more than a three-letter code.
