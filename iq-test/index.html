Under the hood

How the adaptive engine works

Most online IQ tests are fixed questionnaires: everyone answers the same items in the same order, and the score is a simple count of correct answers. AurorIQ works differently. It's built on item response theory (IRT) — the same statistical framework behind computerized adaptive tests like the GRE and the GMAT — using a three-parameter logistic model that accounts for each question's difficulty, how sharply it separates ability levels, and the chance of guessing it correctly.

Here's what that means in practice. The engine maintains a live estimate of your ability from your very first answer. Before each new question, it scans the item bank for the question that would be most informative at your current estimated level — hard enough to challenge you, not so hard that a wrong answer tells it nothing. Answer correctly and the estimate rises, so the next question gets harder. Miss one and it adjusts downward. Over 25 questions, the estimate converges on your level far more efficiently than a fixed test could, because almost no questions are wasted on items far above or below you.

This is also why the test can feel unusually demanding. A fixed test lets strong test-takers coast through easy items; an adaptive test never does. Wherever your ability sits, the engine keeps the questions at your edge — which means nearly everyone, at every level, experiences the test as a genuine stretch. If you're finding it hard, it's working.

Alongside the ability estimate, the engine tracks a standard error — a running measure of how certain it is about your level. That's what powers the 95% confidence interval attached to your final score, and it's a large part of why we describe AurorIQ as an honest instrument: we show you the uncertainty rather than pretending a 15-minute test can produce a perfect number.

What gets measured

Five reasoning domains, one composite

The 25 questions are distributed across five domains of reasoning, and your composite score is assembled from your estimated ability in each:

  • Pattern recognition — abstract sequences and rule discovery: given a series, what governs it, and what comes next? This is the closest cousin to the matrix-style items used in classic fluid-intelligence measures.
  • Numeric reasoning — quantitative relationships, proportions, and number series. Not arithmetic speed, but the ability to see the structure inside numbers.
  • Verbal reasoning — analogies, relationships between meanings, and logical inference expressed in language.
  • Spatial reasoning — mental rotation, folding, and visualizing forms in space without physically manipulating them.
  • Working memory — holding and transforming information over short spans, the mental scratchpad that underpins every other domain.

The balance between your five domain scores is what determines your cognitive archetype. Two people can land on the same composite score with completely different profiles — one carried by spatial and pattern reasoning, another by verbal and memory strength — and the archetype is designed to capture that difference in shape, not just height. For the deeper theory behind the domains, our guides on fluid versus crystallized intelligence and working memory cover the research in plain language.

The reveal

What your result includes

When you finish, the engine resolves your responses into a complete cognitive profile rather than a bare number. You'll see your composite score on the familiar IQ scale (mean 100, standard deviation 15), your percentile — the share of the population your estimate sits above — and the 95% confidence interval around your score. You'll also get a domain breakdown showing your relative strength in each of the five reasoning areas, your archetype, a written report covering strengths, growth areas, and consistency, and a view of exactly where you land on the population curve. If you want to understand what any given band means before you start, the score ranges guide walks through every band and its real-world percentile without the dramatics.

Conditions matter

Getting an accurate result

Cognitive measurement is sensitive to state. The same person can score noticeably differently depending on sleep, stress, and setting — an effect large enough that we wrote a full guide on it. To give the engine a clean signal:

  • Take it in one sitting. The test takes about 15 minutes, and mid-test progress isn't saved. Wait for a window where you won't be interrupted.
  • Don't look things up or use paper. The items are calibrated for unaided reasoning; outside help doesn't raise your ability, it just corrupts the estimate.
  • Answer honestly, not strategically. There's no penalty scheme to game and no pattern to exploit. The engine assumes each answer reflects a genuine attempt.
  • Reasonably rested beats late-night curiosity. Sleep deprivation measurably suppresses working memory and fluid reasoning — the exact things being measured.

Honest by design

What this test is — and isn't

AurorIQ is an educational self-reflection tool. It uses serious psychometric machinery and reports results honestly, including its uncertainty — but it is not a clinical instrument. No online test is. A clinically valid IQ score requires a proctored assessment administered one-on-one by a licensed psychologist using a validated, norm-referenced battery such as the WAIS or Stanford–Binet, and only that kind of score can be used for educational placement, disability evaluation, legal matters, or admission to high-IQ societies such as Mensa.

What an online adaptive test can do is give you a meaningful, well-calibrated estimate of where your reasoning sits on the population curve, and a picture of your cognitive shape across domains — enough to be genuinely informative, provided you read it as an estimate with a confidence interval rather than a verdict. Questions

Frequently asked questions

Do I need to prepare for the IQ test?

No preparation is needed, and practicing beforehand would only distort the estimate. What helps is the setting: a quiet room, a clear 15-minute window, and reasonable alertness. Fatigue, stress, and interruptions are the biggest sources of underperformance on any cognitive test.

Why can't I go back to a previous question?

Because each question is selected based on your answer to the last one, revising an earlier answer would invalidate every question chosen after it. Locking answers keeps the adaptive chain — and therefore the ability estimate — statistically coherent.

Why do the questions feel difficult?

That's the adaptive engine working as intended. It aims questions at the edge of your ability, where your answers carry the most statistical information — so most people answer roughly 50 to 70 percent correctly regardless of their level. Feeling stretched is normal and expected.

What happens if I close the tab mid-test?

Mid-test progress isn't saved — you'd start fresh on your next visit. Once you complete the test, your full result is stored on your device so you can revisit it any time from the results page.

Is my data stored or shared anywhere?

Your answers and results are processed entirely in your browser and saved only to your device's local storage. There's no account, no email, and your responses are never uploaded to a server.

Can I retake the test, and will my score change?

You can retake it whenever you like. Expect some movement between sittings — a few points either way is normal measurement noise, and seeing the same questions again inflates scores slightly. A single sitting under good conditions is the most honest estimate.

What does the confidence interval on my result mean?

Your result includes a 95% confidence interval — the range the engine is statistically confident your true score falls within, given 25 questions of evidence. A shorter test means a wider interval; that range is a more honest reading of your result than the single point estimate.
