How IQ changes with age.

Why scores are age-adjusted in the first place, and how raw reasoning ability actually shifts across a lifetime.

AurorIQ · Updated June 18, 2026 · 5 min read

Scope of this guide: age-normed IQ describes tests with representative age-specific reference samples. AurorIQ has not established those norms. Its age comparisons and score ranges must not be treated as equivalent to a professionally normed test.

A 9-year-old and a 45-year-old can both score 110 on a well-built IQ test. That's not a coincidence, it's the entire point of age-adjusted norms: an IQ score doesn't measure raw performance, it measures how your performance compares to other people roughly your own age.

This creates an answer that surprises almost everyone who searches for "average IQ by age": the average IQ is 100 at every age. For 12-year-olds, for 30-year-olds, for 75-year-olds — 100, by construction. Any table claiming the average 16-year-old has an IQ of 108 or the average 60-year-old has an IQ of 93 misunderstands how the scale works. What genuinely changes with age isn't the average score; it's the raw cognitive machinery underneath it — and that story is far more interesting than a fake table.

Why age-adjustment exists at all

Without it, comparing a child to an adult, or a 25-year-old to a 70-year-old, on the same raw scale would be comparing fundamentally different developmental and cognitive stages. Age-adjusted norms exist to answer a more useful question: relative to your own age group, where do you stand?

Mechanically, test publishers build separate norm tables for each age band. When a 14-year-old and a 40-year-old sit the same test, their raw performances are converted into scores using different reference groups — each is placed on a curve made of their own age peers, re-centered so that the peer average is exactly 100 and the standard deviation is 15. This is why a bright child can outscore an average adult in IQ terms while solving objectively easier problems: the score expresses rarity within an age group, not absolute horsepower. (The full mechanics of the scale are covered in our score ranges guide.)

What actually shifts across a lifetime

While the standardized score stays centered on 100 for any given age group by design, the underlying raw cognitive abilities that feed into it do shift, and not uniformly. As covered in our guide to fluid versus crystallized intelligence, the research generally shows two different trajectories:

  • Fluid reasoning and processing speed tend to peak relatively early, often in the late teens through twenties, with a gradual average decline afterward.
  • Crystallized knowledge and vocabulary tend to hold steady or continue improving well into middle age and beyond, since they're built from accumulated experience rather than raw processing speed.

The decade-by-decade picture, painted with the broad brush the research supports, looks roughly like this:

Ability Typical peak Typical trajectory afterward
Processing speed Late teens–20s Gradual, steady average decline
Fluid reasoning 20s Slow decline, steeper after ~60
Working memory 20s–30s Gradual decline from midlife
Vocabulary 50s–60s Stable or still rising into late life
General knowledge 50s+ Stable well into older age

Directions and approximate timings reflect broad averages from the aging-cognition literature; individual variation around every one of these curves is enormous.

The practical upshot: a person doesn't simply get "less intelligent" as they age in any straightforward sense. The mix of cognitive strengths shifts: less raw processing speed, more accumulated knowledge and pattern recognition built from experience.

What the longitudinal studies actually show

There's an important wrinkle in how this research gets done, and it changes the headline. Early cross-sectional studies — testing 20-year-olds and 60-year-olds at the same moment and comparing them — painted a bleak picture of steep, early decline. But cross-sectional comparisons confound age with generation: the 60-year-olds grew up with different schooling, nutrition, and test familiarity than the 20-year-olds, so part of the "decline" was really a cohort difference.

Longitudinal work, which follows the same people across decades — most famously the Seattle Longitudinal Study, which has tracked thousands of adults since 1956 — tells a gentler story. Followed within themselves, most people hold their reasoning abilities remarkably stable through their 40s and 50s, with meaningful average decline in most domains arriving later than the cross-sectional data implied. Decline is real, but it starts later, moves slower, and varies far more between individuals than the popular "downhill after 25" framing suggests.

The Flynn effect: the average moves under your feet

One more force complicates any talk of "average IQ": raw performance on IQ-style tasks rose substantially across the twentieth century — roughly three points per decade in many industrialized countries. This is the Flynn effect, and it means a person scoring exactly 100 today would outscore the average test-taker of 1950 by a wide margin on the same raw items.

Test publishers absorb this by periodically re-norming: the average is dragged back to 100 against a fresh, contemporary sample. So the "average IQ" never rises in published terms even as raw performance shifts — another reason the number 100 is a moving anchor rather than a fixed quantity of ability. (In several countries the effect has plateaued or partially reversed in recent decades; the causes of both the rise and the stall remain genuinely contested in the literature.)

Childhood development is a separate story

In children, "mental age" relative to chronological age was the original basis of the IQ ratio before deviation-based scoring took over. A child's reasoning ability develops rapidly and somewhat unevenly, which is part of why childhood IQ scores are generally considered less stable predictors of adult ability than scores taken in adulthood, where reasoning ability has largely stabilized.

The stability numbers make the point concretely. Scores from testing before age five or six correlate only weakly with adult scores; by late childhood the correlation strengthens considerably, and from adolescence onward scores become quite stable — though never perfectly so. A famous Scottish cohort retested at age 77 on the same test they'd taken at age 11 showed a strong but far-from-perfect correlation, meaning plenty of individuals moved substantially in both directions across a lifetime. Two implications follow: an early childhood score should be held very loosely, and even a stable adult score describes a position that individual lives can and do depart from.

What this means for comparing your own results over time

If you retake a cognitive assessment years apart, don't expect, or worry about, your raw score staying perfectly static. Day-to-day factors aside, the underlying mix of fluid and crystallized ability you're drawing on genuinely shifts over a life. A meaningful comparison looks at the trend and the percentile band, not a single point-to-point difference.

What this means for test-takers

If you're interpreting your own score on AurorIQ or any other cognitive assessment, it helps to keep age-related trajectories in mind. A score taken in your twenties reflects peak fluid reasoning capacity — the raw pattern-recognition and working-memory power that IQ tests lean most heavily on. The same person tested at forty-five may score a few points lower on those fluid subtests, not because they've become less intelligent in any practical sense, but because the biological hardware underlying rapid novel problem-solving has shifted slightly.

Conversely, crystallized intelligence — the accumulated knowledge, vocabulary, and domain expertise that comes from decades of learning and experience — typically increases well into your fifties and sixties. Most IQ tests, including AurorIQ, weight fluid reasoning more heavily than crystallized knowledge, which means they tend to underestimate the overall intellectual competence of experienced older adults. For a deeper dive into this distinction, read our guide on fluid versus crystallized intelligence.

The practical takeaway: don't treat a single IQ score as a fixed, lifelong number. It's a snapshot of specific cognitive abilities measured under specific conditions at a specific point in your life. The confidence interval reported alongside your AurorIQ result acknowledges this uncertainty explicitly, and that honesty is by design.

Common questions

Does IQ go down as you get older?

Your IQ score itself, being relative to an age-matched comparison group, doesn't automatically decline just because you age. The underlying raw fluid-reasoning ability that feeds into it does tend to decline on average after early adulthood, but crystallized knowledge tends to offset much of that for everyday functioning.

What is the average IQ for a 12-year-old (or any age)?

100 — the same as for every other age, by design. Scores are normed within age groups, so each group's average is re-centered to 100. Tables claiming different average IQs for different ages misread how the scale is constructed.

Does the Flynn effect mean people are getting smarter?

Raw scores on IQ-type tasks rose through the twentieth century, but whether that reflects genuine gains in underlying intelligence — versus schooling, test familiarity, nutrition, and more abstract thinking habits — is still debated. In several countries the effect has recently plateaued or reversed.

At what age does IQ peak?

Different cognitive abilities peak at different ages. Raw processing speed and fluid reasoning tend to peak earlier, often in the late teens to twenties, while vocabulary and accumulated knowledge can continue improving into middle age and beyond.
