Learning Science · Study Methods

How to study effectively: what the science says.

The methods that feel most productive — rereading, highlighting, cramming — are among the weakest. Here's what decades of research show actually builds lasting knowledge, and how to switch.

AurorIQ · Updated July 21, 2026 · 10 min read

There's a cruel irony at the centre of how most people study: the techniques that feel the most productive are, on the whole, the least effective. Rereading a chapter until it flows smoothly, highlighting in three colours, marathon cramming the night before — these dominate because they feel like progress in the moment. Meanwhile the methods that genuinely build durable knowledge often feel slower, harder, and more frustrating while you're doing them. Learning how to study effectively is largely a matter of learning to distrust that feeling.

This isn't folk wisdom. Cognitive scientists have spent decades comparing study techniques head to head, and the findings are unusually consistent. A landmark 2013 review by Dunlosky and colleagues ranked ten common techniques by the strength of their evidence, and the results embarrassed a lot of study habits: the two most powerful methods were among the least popular, while the most popular — rereading and highlighting — landed near the bottom.

Why "it feels like it's working" misleads you

The root problem is a distinction the psychologist Robert Bjork has spent a career on: learning is not the same as performance. Performance is how well you can do something right now, during study. Learning is the durable change that lets you do it later, under different conditions. The two often move in opposite directions. Conditions that make study feel easy and fluent — rereading, massing everything together, staying on one topic — tend to inflate performance in the moment while doing little for lasting learning. Conditions that feel harder — testing yourself, spacing sessions out, mixing topics — depress in-the-moment performance but produce far better retention. Bjork calls these desirable difficulties: struggle that feels like failure but is actually where the learning happens.

This is why rereading is so seductive and so weak. The second and third pass feel effortless, and we read that fluency as mastery. But fluency only means you recognise the material when it's in front of you. It says almost nothing about whether you can recall and use it when it isn't — which is exactly what an exam, or real life, demands. Psychologists call this gap the illusion of competence, and it's the single biggest reason capable people underperform: they stop studying a topic the moment it feels familiar, mistaking recognition for knowledge.

The one-sentence version: if a study session feels smooth and easy, be suspicious. Effective studying usually feels effortful, because the effort of retrieving and reconstructing knowledge is the thing that strengthens it.

The two heavyweights: retrieval and spacing

If you change only two things about how you study, change these.

Retrieval practice — testing yourself from memory — is the most powerful single technique in the research, full stop. The act of pulling information out of your head, rather than putting it back in by rereading, is what consolidates it. This is the testing effect: students who study a passage and then take a practice test remember far more weeks later than students who spend the same time restudying the passage, even though the restudiers feel more confident. In practice, retrieval means closing the book and writing down everything you remember, using flashcards, answering practice questions, or explaining a concept aloud without looking. Every time you make yourself reconstruct an idea from memory, you strengthen the path back to it. The struggle to remember isn't a sign the method is failing; it's the mechanism.

Spaced practice — distributing study across time instead of massing it — is the close second. The spacing effect is one of the oldest and most robust findings in all of psychology: the same total study time produces dramatically better long-term retention when spread across many days than when crammed into one block. Reviewing material after you've partly forgotten it forces a harder, more valuable retrieval than reviewing it while it's still fresh. Cramming can get you through tomorrow's exam, but the knowledge evaporates within days; spacing builds something that lasts. The practical move is simple and unglamorous: start earlier and study in shorter sessions across more days, revisiting each topic at growing intervals. The supporting cast: interleaving and elaboration

Two more techniques earn solid, if slightly more situational, support.

Interleaving means mixing different topics or problem types within a study session rather than blocking one to exhaustion before moving on. Studying all your algebra, then all your geometry, feels orderly; shuffling them together feels messy and harder. But the messiness is the point — interleaving forces you to repeatedly work out which approach a problem calls for, which is precisely the skill an exam tests, and blocked practice never builds. The classic demonstration had students learn to calculate the volumes of different solids: those who practised the types blocked did better during practice but far worse on a later test than those who interleaved. Once again, the harder-feeling method won where it counted.

Elaboration — and its cousin self-explanation — means processing material for meaning rather than surface: asking yourself why a fact is true and how it connects to what you already know, and explaining ideas in your own words instead of memorising them flat. "Why would that be the case?" is a deceptively powerful question, because generating an explanation forces you to build the connections that make knowledge retrievable and transferable. Deep, meaning-focused processing consistently beats shallow, verbatim processing.

What to drop (or demote)

The flip side of knowing what works is being honest about what doesn't. Rereading and highlighting are the two great time-sinks. Both feel productive, both are nearly ubiquitous, and both rank near the bottom of the evidence. Highlighting is arguably worse than neutral: it can actively hurt, because the act of marking a passage substitutes for the harder work of understanding it, and a page glowing with yellow gives a comforting but false sense of having "done" something. Neither is worthless as a first pass — you have to read the material once — but they should be the start of studying, not the whole of it. The moment a chapter feels familiar, that's your cue to close it and start retrieving.

Massed cramming is the third to demote. It isn't useless — a last-minute session can genuinely lift tomorrow's score — but it buys short-term performance at the cost of long-term learning, and it crowds out the spacing that would have served you far better had you started earlier. Treat it as an emergency tool, not a strategy.

The learning-styles myth

One idea deserves special mention because it's so widespread and so wrong: the belief that each of us has a "learning style" — visual, auditory, kinesthetic — and that studying in our preferred style improves learning. It's intuitive, it's everywhere in schools, and the evidence for it is essentially nonexistent. The specific claim that matters, the meshing hypothesis, is that matching how material is presented to a learner's style improves their learning. When researchers have actually tested that prediction, they've repeatedly failed to find it. A widely cited 2008 review concluded there was no adequate evidence to justify building teaching around learning styles at all.

This matters practically, because believing you're a "visual learner" can quietly hold you back — steering you away from reading dense text or explaining ideas aloud, exactly the varied practice that builds flexible understanding. The more useful question is never "what kind of learner am I?" but "what does this particular material demand, and am I studying it in a way that will still work under pressure?" The techniques above work for essentially everyone, no style required. Putting it together: how to actually study a topic

Here's what the evidence looks like in practice. Do a first pass to understand the material — read it, and yes, highlight sparingly if it helps you engage. Then, crucially, close the book and retrieve: write down everything you remember, or answer questions on it, and only then check what you missed. Space your reviews across days rather than piling them up, revisiting each topic as it starts to fade. When you have several topics, interleave them rather than finishing one before starting the next. Throughout, keep asking why and how, and explain ideas in your own words. And protect the conditions that make any of this possible: attention and sleep set the ceiling on everything above, which is why a rested, focused hour beats three distracted ones — and why sleep and stress affect performance more than most people expect.

None of this requires more hours; in fact it often requires fewer, because you stop pouring time into rereading that wasn't working. What it requires is tolerating the discomfort of methods that feel harder in the moment because they're doing more. That trade — a little more effort now for a lot more retention later — is the whole of effective studying. Common questions

What is the single most effective study technique?

Retrieval practice — testing yourself from memory instead of rereading. Across hundreds of studies it produces the largest, most reliable gains in long-term retention of any single technique. Closing the book and trying to reproduce what you learned, then checking, does far more than passively reviewing the same material again.

Why does rereading feel effective if it isn't?

Because it produces fluency — the material feels familiar and easy the second and third time through, and we mistake that familiarity for mastery. This is the 'illusion of competence'. Familiarity means you recognise the material when you see it, which is very different from being able to recall and use it when it isn't in front of you.

Do learning styles matter for studying?

No credible evidence supports the idea that matching instruction to a 'visual', 'auditory', or 'kinesthetic' style improves learning. Reviews of the research have repeatedly failed to find the effect the theory predicts. What reliably helps is using effective techniques — retrieval, spacing, elaboration — which work well for essentially everyone regardless of any supposed style.

How far in advance should I start studying for an exam?

As early as is practical, so you can space your reviews. The same total study time produces markedly better retention when distributed across many days than when massed into a few long sessions right before the exam. Starting early isn't about doing more work; it's about spreading the same work so the spacing effect can do its job.
