Career · Choosing a Path

How to choose a career: a practical framework.

"Follow your passion" is terrible advice on its own. Fit is really three questions, not one — what draws you, what you need, and how you like to work. Here's how to use all three.

AurorIQ · Updated July 21, 2026 · 10 min read

The standard career advice — "follow your passion" — is not just unhelpful but actively misleading, because it collapses a genuinely multi-dimensional decision into a single feeling. Passion, in the sense most people mean it, is roughly what career psychologists call an interest: the kind of work that draws you. And interest is real and important. But it's one of at least three distinct dimensions of career fit, and a job that nails your interests while ignoring the other two can still make you miserable. Choosing well means asking three different questions, not one.

Those three questions map onto three bodies of research, and the useful insight is that they're separable: they can point in the same direction or pull against each other, and knowing which is happening is most of the value. The three are your interests (what draws you), your values (what you need from work to be satisfied), and your work style (how you prefer to operate day to day). Let's take them in turn, then talk about how to use them together.

Lens one: interests — what draws you

Interests are the most familiar lens, and the one "follow your passion" is really gesturing at. The dominant framework here is the one developed by the psychologist John Holland, usually called RIASEC after its six themes: Realistic (hands-on, practical, physical work), Investigative (analytical, scientific, problem-solving), Artistic (creative, expressive, unstructured), Social (helping, teaching, caring), Enterprising (leading, persuading, selling), and Conventional (organising, detail, structure). Most people are a blend of two or three themes rather than a pure type, and the combination — your "Holland code" — points toward families of work where the day-to-day activities are likely to engage you.

The crucial thing to understand about interests is what they are not: they are not aptitude. An interest inventory measures what activities appeal to you, not whether you'd be good at them. You can be fascinated by architecture and struggle with the spatial and mathematical demands of it; you can be a natural at something that leaves you cold. Interest and ability are correlated but genuinely distinct, and conflating them is a classic error. A good interest result gives you directions to explore, not a promise of talent. Our career interest profiler maps your RIASEC themes for exactly this purpose — as a starting map, honestly framed as interests rather than aptitude.

Lens two: values — what you need to be satisfied

If interests tell you what work would engage you, values tell you what a job must provide for you to be content in it. This is the lens most people neglect, and it's the one that most often explains why someone in a field they were genuinely drawn to still feels hollow. The research tradition here is the Theory of Work Adjustment, which underpins the U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET work-importance framework, and it identifies six core work values: achievement (using your abilities, seeing results), independence (autonomy over how you work), recognition (status and advancement), relationships (friendly colleagues, service to others), support (fair, capable management), and working conditions (security, pay, comfort, variety).

What makes values powerful as a lens is that they predict satisfaction better than interests do. Interests get you in the door of a field; values determine whether you can stand it once you're there. A creatively fascinating role at a chaotic company with no security can be a worse fit for someone who deeply values stability than a duller role that provides it. Because values are about needs rather than pulls, they also expose trade-offs cleanly, which we'll come back to. Our work values profiler ranks these six needs for you, so you can see which ones a given path would satisfy and which it would starve.

The neglected lens: if you only have time for one assessment beyond interests, make it values. Most career regret isn't "I picked a field I found boring" — it's "I picked a field that couldn't give me the autonomy, or security, or human connection I needed, and I didn't realise that was the thing that mattered until years in."

Lens three: work style — how you like to operate

The third lens is more granular and often overlooked entirely: not what field you're in or what it provides, but how you prefer to work day to day. Do you do your best thinking alone or by bouncing ideas off people? Do you want a clear plan and structure, or room to improvise? Deep focus on one thing, or variety and task-switching? A steady pace, or intense sprints against deadlines? These preferences, drawn from organizational psychology, aren't abilities and none of them is better than its opposite — but the fit between your style and a role's daily texture has a large effect on whether the work feels natural or exhausting.

Two people can share the same interests and the same values and still thrive in very different roles because of style. The collaborative improviser and the independent planner might both be drawn to, say, product design and both value autonomy — but one belongs on a fast, social team and the other in a focused, self-directed niche. Style is what distinguishes between two jobs that look identical on paper. Our work style profiler maps these preferences as spectrums, with no type and no "correct" profile — just a clearer read on the conditions that suit you.

Using the three together

The real power comes from overlaying the three lenses, because that's where fit — and the honest trade-offs — become visible. The ideal is a path that sits in the overlap of all three: work whose activities engage your interests, whose conditions meet your values, and whose daily rhythm suits your style. When a path lands in that overlap, it tends to feel not just tolerable but right.

More often, though, the three don't perfectly align, and that misalignment is the most useful thing the framework surfaces. A field you're fascinated by (interest) may be structurally unable to provide the security you need (value) — the classic tension for people drawn to the arts, academia, or startups. A role that satisfies your values may demand a work style that drains you. These aren't reasons to give up; they're the actual decision, made explicit. Naming the trade-off — "this path gives me the creative work I want but asks me to accept less stability than I'd like" — lets you decide with your eyes open, and often points to a smarter move: finding the corner of a field that resolves the tension, rather than abandoning the field or walking in blind.

A simple way to run the exercise: profile all three, then look for the pattern. Where do they agree? That's your strongest signal — lean into paths that hit all three. Where do they conflict? That's your key decision — which of your top values are you willing to trade, and for how long? And where are you uncertain? That's what to go investigate through conversations, internships, and real exposure, because no assessment substitutes for contact with the actual work.

The honest limits of any career assessment

It's worth being blunt about what these tools can and can't do, because the career-test industry oversells relentlessly. Every one of these lenses is self-report: it captures how you see yourself right now, which is genuinely useful but also shifts with life stage, mood, and experience. The person you are at twenty-two wants different things than the person you'll be at thirty-five, and your results will move accordingly. No test can weigh your specific circumstances — your finances, your location, your obligations, the opportunities actually in front of you — and none can predict how you'll grow into a role you can't yet imagine.

So treat any assessment, including ours, as a structured way to think, not an oracle that decides. Its real value is in narrowing a paralysing field of options to a handful worth exploring, and in surfacing questions you hadn't articulated — "wait, how much do I actually value independence?" A good result should make you more curious and more specific, not hand you a job title. The Career hub brings all three profilers together for exactly this kind of triangulation, and the honest answer to "what should I do with my life?" is always the same: use the map to narrow the search, then go touch the real thing.

Common questions

Can a test tell me what career to choose?

No, and be wary of any that claims to. Career assessments map your interests, values, and preferences to give you structured directions to explore, but they can't weigh your circumstances, opportunities, or how you'll grow. Treat them as a map that narrows the field and sparks better questions, not a verdict that hands you an answer.

What's the difference between interests and values in career choice?

Interests are what kinds of work draw you — the activities you'd enjoy doing. Values are what you need from a job to feel satisfied, such as independence, security, recognition, or helping others. You can be genuinely interested in a field that still makes you miserable because it clashes with your values, which is why the two lenses answer different questions.

Do interest tests measure whether I'd be good at a job?

No. Interest inventories measure what draws you, not your aptitude for it. Interest and ability are related but distinct — you can be fascinated by a field you'd struggle in, or capable at something that bores you. A good career decision considers both, plus your values and how you like to work.

Why do interests and values sometimes conflict?

Because they're genuinely separate dimensions. Someone drawn to creative work (an interest) who also needs financial security (a value) faces a real tension, not a contradiction. Naming the conflict is the point — it shows you exactly what trade-off a given path asks of you, so you can decide with your eyes open rather than being blindsided later.
