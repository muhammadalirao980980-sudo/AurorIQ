Improving Cognition

Sleep, stress, and test performance.

A bad night's sleep can move your score more than most people realize. Here's what actually shifts performance on a given day.

AurorIQ Editorial Team · Updated June 20, 2026 · 5 min read

Your score on any given day reflects two things tangled together: your underlying reasoning ability, and the state you happened to be in while taking the test. The second part gets far less attention than it deserves, and it can move a result by more than most people expect.

Psychometricians have a name for this second part: state factors, as opposed to trait ability. Trait ability is the relatively stable thing an IQ test is trying to estimate. State factors — sleep, stress, mood, alertness, caffeine, illness, even the room you're in — are the noise layered on top of it on any given day. A well-built test reports a confidence interval precisely because it can't fully separate the two from a single sitting. The good news is that state factors are largely under your control, and managing them is the closest thing there is to legitimately "improving" your score: you're not raising your ceiling, you're just removing the things dragging you below it.

Sleep deprivation is one of the best-documented factors

The research on sleep deprivation and cognitive performance is some of the most consistently replicated in the field. Sleep loss reliably impairs sustained attention, working memory, and processing speed — three abilities that feed directly into performance on most reasoning tasks. A single poor night's sleep before a test is a genuinely meaningful confound, not a minor footnote.

The mechanism worth understanding is attentional lapses. Sleep-deprivation studies using sustained-attention tasks find that performance degrades less through a uniform slowing and more through brief microlapses — moments where attention simply drops out. On a timed reasoning test, a lapse at the wrong moment costs you an item you'd otherwise solve, and because an adaptive test uses each answer to steer the next question, a lapse-driven miss can nudge the whole difficulty trajectory downward. Two dimensions matter independently: total sleep debt built up over several nights, and acute deprivation the night before. Both degrade the same abilities, and chronic partial restriction — a run of six-hour nights — accumulates deficits that people routinely fail to notice in themselves, having adapted to feeling foggy as normal.

The practical implication is narrow but firm: a test taken after an all-nighter, deep in jet lag, or at the tail of a bad sleep week is measuring your sleep-deprived state as much as your ability. The number is real; it just isn't the number you're looking for.

Acute stress cuts both ways

Stress and cognitive performance have a more complicated relationship than "stress is bad." A moderate amount of arousal can sharpen focus and improve performance up to a point, a relationship researchers have long described with an inverted-U shape: too little arousal and you're sluggish, too much and performance falls off as anxiety starts consuming the same mental resources you need for reasoning.

Test anxiety specifically: research on test anxiety suggests it can consume working-memory capacity that would otherwise go toward the actual reasoning task, meaning someone capable of solving a problem under calm conditions might genuinely struggle with the identical problem while anxious, independent of their baseline ability.

This is the leading account of test anxiety: it's a competition for working memory. The worried inner monologue — am I doing badly, what does this say about me, I should be faster than this — runs on the same limited mental workspace that the reasoning task needs. Since working memory is one of the strongest correlates of reasoning performance, anything that occupies it during the test taxes the exact resource being measured. It's also why the effect hits the hardest problems worst: easy items leave enough spare capacity to absorb the distraction, while demanding items need all of it.

The inverted-U has a practical read. You don't want zero arousal — a completely flat, indifferent state leaves you sluggish and careless. You want the modest, focused alertness of caring about doing well without the spiral of dreading failure. For most people that means treating the test as interesting rather than high-stakes, which for a self-directed online assessment is a genuinely available reframe: nothing rides on it, so there's no failure to dread.

Environment and familiarity matter too

Noise, interruptions, an unfamiliar test format, and simple practice effects (having seen a similar test type before) all introduce variation that has nothing to do with underlying reasoning ability. This is part of why a single test session, taken once, under one set of conditions, should be read as one data point rather than a definitive, permanent measurement.

Caffeine, food, and the smaller levers

A few second-order factors round out the picture. Caffeine at a habitual dose reliably restores alertness and sustained attention, especially useful if you're mildly under-slept — but it follows the same inverted-U as stress, and an unusually large dose can add jitter and anxiety that cost more than the alertness gains. If you drink coffee, a normal amount is fine; test day is not the day to double it or, if you never drink it, to start. Blood sugar matters at the extremes rather than the margins: taking a demanding cognitive test ravenously hungry adds a distraction and a mild performance drag, so a normal meal beforehand helps, while there's no evidence that loading up on "brain foods" does anything special. Hydration works the same way — being noticeably dehydrated impairs attention; being extra-hydrated confers no bonus.

What this means practically

If you want your result to reflect your reasoning ability as accurately as a single session reasonably can, a rested, calm, distraction-free environment will get you closer to that than a tired, stressed, or rushed one. It won't change who you are — for that, explore your personality profile — but it will change how well that day's number reflects you.

Practical recommendations for test-takers

If you want your AurorIQ result to reflect your genuine cognitive ability as accurately as possible, the research on sleep and stress points to a few straightforward guidelines. Take the test after a normal night of sleep — not after an all-nighter, not when jet-lagged, and not late at night when your circadian rhythm is pushing you toward sleep. Most people's fluid reasoning peaks in the late morning or early afternoon, though individual chronotypes vary.

Minimise acute stress before the test. This doesn't mean you need to meditate for an hour — just avoid taking it immediately after a stressful meeting, a difficult conversation, or while you're ruminating about something unrelated. The goal is to give your working memory its full capacity for the task at hand, rather than having part of it occupied by emotional processing.

Find a quiet environment with minimal interruptions. Each question on AurorIQ builds on your accumulated performance estimate, and the adaptive engine selects items optimised for your current ability level. A distraction mid-test can cause you to miss a question you would have answered correctly under normal conditions, which shifts the difficulty trajectory for subsequent items. The confidence interval in your result already accounts for some of this noise, but reducing environmental interference gives you the most accurate snapshot of your actual ability.

Common questions

Can lack of sleep lower your IQ test score?

Sleep deprivation reliably impairs attention, working memory, and processing speed in the short term, which can measurably lower performance on a cognitive test taken while sleep-deprived, even though it doesn't reflect a change in your underlying baseline ability.

How much can stress or sleep actually change my score?

There's no single fixed figure, but the state factors discussed here operate within the range of ordinary test-retest variation — several points either way is entirely plausible from a bad night's sleep or high anxiety alone. That's enough to shift your percentile noticeably near the middle of the distribution, while rarely moving your broad band.

Does caffeine help on a cognitive test?

At a habitual dose it reliably restores alertness and attention, which helps most if you're mildly under-slept. An unusually large dose can backfire by adding jitter and anxiety. Test day isn't the time to change your normal caffeine routine in either direction.

Can I "practice" my way to a higher score?

Repeating the same or similar tests produces a practice effect — a modest score bump from format familiarity, not from raised ability. It fades once the format stops being novel and doesn't transfer to genuinely new problem types, which is why a first, well-rested sitting is the most honest estimate.

Is it better to take a cognitive test in the morning or evening?

Research on time-of-day effects on cognitive performance is mixed and appears to interact with individual chronotype, whether someone is naturally more alert earlier or later in the day, rather than pointing to one universally optimal testing time.
