Learning · ~3 minutes

Can you hold your attention?

Press on the circles, hold back on the squares. Simple — until the squares turn rare and the habit to press takes over. This Go/No-Go task measures vigilance, impulse control, and how steady your focus stays over forty trials.

● Circle — press ■ Square — hold back

Forty shapes will flash one at a time. Press the panel (or Space) the instant you see a circle. When you see a square, do nothing and let it pass. Squares are rare, so the urge to press everything is the whole challenge. Around three minutes.

About this instrument

What this measures — and what it doesn't

  • Measures

    Sustained attention

    Two things at once: staying vigilant across a repetitive stream, and inhibiting a prepotent response when the rare stop-signal appears. Together they form the core of what everyday language calls focus.

  • Method

    Go/No-Go, 40 trials

    Circles (go, ~75%) and squares (no-go, ~25%) flash for one second each. The high go-rate builds a response habit, so withholding on squares is genuinely effortful — which is exactly what makes commission errors informative.

  • Result

    A profile, not one number

    Accuracy, impulse errors, missed signals, mean reaction time, and consistency — plus an approximate composite that rewards steadiness. The shape of the profile tells you whether your focus fails by impulsivity or by drifting off.

  • Limits

    Honest limits

    This is not an ADHD test or any clinical assessment. Go/No-Go performance is highly state-dependent — sleep, caffeine, noise, and motivation all move it — and one short run is a snapshot, not a trait. Real attention concerns belong with a clinician.

Questions

Before you begin

What does this focus test measure?

Sustained attention and response inhibition, using a Go/No-Go task. You respond quickly to common 'go' signals and withhold on rare 'no-go' signals. It captures how well you stay vigilant over time and resist acting on impulse.

What do commission and omission errors mean?

A commission error is pressing when you should have withheld — a failure of impulse control. An omission error is missing a signal you should have responded to — a lapse of attention. The two patterns say different things about how your focus breaks down.

Why does response consistency matter?

A steady reaction time signals stable attention; a reaction time that swings wildly between fast and slow is one of the clearest markers of a wandering mind — often more telling than average speed. That's why the score weighs consistency, not just accuracy.

Is this an ADHD test?

No. Go/No-Go tasks resemble tools used in attention research, but this is an educational instrument, not a diagnostic one. A low score can come from tiredness, a distracting environment, or simply an off day. If attention affects your daily life, that's a conversation for a qualified clinician.
